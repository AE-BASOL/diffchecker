\begin{abstract}
ML-KEM (FIPS~203) is the standardised post-quantum key encapsulation mechanism.
This study investigates whether the Fujisaki--Okamoto (FO) implicit-rejection
guard in the \texttt{mlkem-native} decapsulation path leaks ACCEPT/REJECT timing
information. The natural hypothesis, that the conditional-select
guard \texttt{mlk\_ct\_cmov\_zero} acts as a branch-predictor oracle, is first falsified:
across seven compiler configurations (GCC and Clang, \texttt{-O2/-O3/-Os}),
the guard compiles to branchless assembly. No branch is present in any configuration.
A repeatable full-path timing separation nonetheless persists. Measured under an
identical fixed-data, balanced protocol on the same source and compiler, the
separation is microarchitecture-generation dependent: on an Intel Westmere core
(1st-generation Core, circa 2010) ACCEPT is measurably slower than REJECT, exhibiting
a statistically significant penalty of approximately 80 cycles,
whereas on an Intel Raptor Lake core (14th-generation, 2023) no repeatable separation
is observed. The microarchitecture generation determines the outcome.
Per-iteration performance counters show Westmere incurs a 58-fold higher L1 data-cache
miss rate than Raptor Lake, with ACCEPT producing a directionally consistent excess of
misses on Westmere. The timing
behaviour is therefore consistent with a data-dependent L1D cache-pressure effect in
the ciphertext-decompression path, exposed on older cores but masked on modern ones.
This is the first cross-generation
characterisation of ML-KEM decapsulation timing on x86.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
%==============================================================================
Post-quantum cryptography has moved from research to standardisation: in 2024 NIST
finalised ML-KEM as the post-quantum key encapsulation mechanism (KEM) under
FIPS~203~\cite{nist_fips_203}, and adoption in TLS~1.3, VPNs, and hardware security
modules is accelerating. While ML-KEM's mathematical security is well studied, the
side-channel resistance of concrete implementations determines whether confidentiality
guarantees hold in practice. Timing attacks, introduced by Kocher~\cite{kocher1996timing},
remain a practical threat whenever execution time varies with secret-dependent data.

A fundamental tension exists between source-level constant-time guarantees and
the microarchitectural behaviour they enforce. Code written to
avoid secret-dependent branches may still exhibit timing variation: processors
execute constant-time constructs through pipelines, caches, and predictors whose
timing is not specified by the instruction-set architecture (ISA). This gap is the target
of the present study, instantiated at the FO-transform guard in ML-KEM decapsulation.
This paper characterises that gap.

ML-KEM \texttt{Decaps} calls decrypt, re-encrypt, and compare (Figure~\ref{fig:fo-flow});
on mismatch the
Fujisaki--Okamoto implicit-rejection rule invokes the conditional-select guard,
which returns a pseudo-random value rather than the true shared secret. The
data-dependent step this study examines is the ciphertext-decompression routine,
whose reconstructed coefficient values vary with the ciphertext even when its
write indices are stride-regular. The natural hypothesis is that the conditional-select
guard is a branch-predictor oracle: it compiles to a conditional branch whose predictor
state differs between ACCEPT and REJECT. Analysis of the assembly confirmed the absence
of a branch. This observation directs the investigation toward architectural mechanisms
to understand what makes the paths distinguishable and on which hardware.

In this study, the FO-transform guard is first evaluated to determine whether it
compiles to branchless assembly across all tested compiler configurations. Building on this
foundation, the presence of a statistically significant ACCEPT/REJECT timing separation
is measured in ML-KEM decapsulation under controlled conditions. Finally, the analysis investigates whether
a timing separation observed on a legacy Intel core (Westmere) persists on a
current-generation core (Raptor Lake) under an identical protocol.

Four primary contributions are made by this study:
\begin{description}
  \item[Branchless guard] The FO-transform rejection guard compiles to branchless assembly across all seven tested compiler configurations, ruling out a timing side-channel via the branch predictor.
  \item[Westmere separation] A repeatable ACCEPT/REJECT timing separation is reported on Westmere.
  \item[Generation dependence] The separation is generation-dependent, vanishing on Raptor Lake, a finding corroborated by a significant L1D-miss-rate gap.
\end{description}

\section{Background}\label{sec:background}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{ML-KEM, the FO Transform, and Constant-Time}
ML-KEM is built from an IND-CPA-secure public-key encryption scheme over module lattices
(Module-LWE), wrapped by a Fujisaki--Okamoto (FO) transform that yields IND-CCA2 security:
\begin{equation}
  R_q = \mathbb{Z}_q[X]/(X^n+1), \quad n=256,\; q=3329,\; k=3 .
\end{equation}
During decapsulation the recipient decrypts, re-encrypts, and compares; on mismatch the FO
implicit-rejection rule returns a pseudo-random key via the conditional-select guard.
This selection is implemented as constant-time conditional-select code rather than a
data-dependent branch, to avoid leaking the comparison result. The security argument
assumes this constant-time property holds through compilation and microarchitectural execution,
not only in the source.

The \texttt{polyvec\_decompress\_du} routine reconstructs polynomial coefficients from the
$d_u{=}10$-bit packed ciphertext. Its write indices are stride-regular, fixed over
coefficient positions independent of the data. The reconstructed coefficient
values are data-dependent. Downstream NTT steps read these values, so the memory
working set varies with the ciphertext at the level of values, not addresses. This
data-dependent memory-access pattern is what the present study examines.
The reconstructed coefficient values are ciphertext-dependent; consequently, the
set of memory locations read by downstream NTT operations varies between ACCEPT
and REJECT ciphertexts.
This variation in the accessed working set is consistent with a data-dependent
L1D utilisation asymmetry, in which one path more frequently approaches the
capacity boundary of the private data cache than the other.

\subsection{Microarchitecture and Cache-Generation Dependence}
Whether a constant-time source construct executes in constant time depends on the
microarchitecture, which the ISA leaves unspecified. Two processor generations may run the
same branchless code yet show markedly different memory-system behaviour. Key axes of variation include
private L1 data-cache capacity (Westmere: 32\,KB; Raptor Lake: 48\,KB), hardware-prefetch
aggressiveness, and the depth of the out-of-order execution window (roughly 96 entries on Westmere versus 512 on Raptor Lake~\cite{intel_sdm}). On an older architecture with a smaller cache and a narrower execution window, a
data-dependent working set can more readily reach capacity pressure, forcing pipeline stalls. Conversely, a newer architecture with a larger cache, more sophisticated replacement policies, and aggressive prefetching can absorb and mask the same memory-access pattern without exposing a timing differential.

This creates a source-versus-microarchitecture gap. A routine that is rigorously verified as constant-time in source code may still interact with the cache hierarchy in a
data-dependent way that becomes visible on some generations but remains entirely hidden on others. Determining whether such a gap
produces an observable, statistically significant timing asymmetry—and identifying the specific hardware on which it manifests—is the core question this study addresses. By comparing two microarchitectures separated by over a decade, this research isolates the generation-dependent component of timing leakage.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}
%==============================================================================
Prior work addresses timing side channels in lattice KEMs primarily at the source or
compiler level, or on a single platform. Table~\ref{tab:related} positions the present
study against the closest related work along five axes.
GoFetch~\cite{chen2024gofetch} demonstrates active DMP-driven key recovery on Apple Silicon;
the present study, by contrast, passively characterises timing separation across x86
microarchitecture generations without demonstrating an attack.

Classic cache-timing analysis~\cite{bernstein2005cache,osvik2006cache,percival2005cache}
and cross-platform PQC benchmarking~\cite{kannwischer2019pqm4} establish the measurement
primitives but do not compare Intel microarchitecture generations on the same ML-KEM codebase.
The mechanism is consistent with L1D capacity pressure (32\,KB vs.\ 48\,KB;
${\sim}58{\times}$ miss-rate difference).
On Westmere, a separation persists across all tested compiler configurations
($d{=}{+}0.22$--${+}0.39$, public API); on Raptor Lake no repeatable separation is
observed ($|d|{<}0.016$).
Attribution stops at ``consistent with''; no causal claim is advanced.

\section{Methodology}\label{sec:method}
%==============================================================================
\subsection{Target and platforms}
All experiments use stock \texttt{mlkem-native} v1.1.0 (commit \texttt{d2cae2b}), the
ML-KEM-768 parameter set, with \texttt{kem.c}/\texttt{indcpa.c}/\texttt{verify.c}
unmodified. The public decapsulation API is measured. Table~\ref{tab:platforms} lists the
platforms. The two x86 cores, Westmere (i3-M380) and Raptor Lake (i9-14900K), are the
generation pair central to RQ3.



Across seven compiler configurations (GCC and Clang at \texttt{-O2}/\texttt{-O3}/\texttt{-Os}),
disassembly of \texttt{mlk\_ct\_cmov\_zero} contains no conditional branch.
No source-level branch effect is possible.

\subsection{Fixed-data protocol}
Random per-iteration inputs inject NTT-related cache noise that swamps the signal. A
deterministic fixed-data protocol is employed: \texttt{keypair\_derand} and \texttt{enc\_derand} with
\texttt{seed=0} yield a single reproducible key-ciphertext tuple, alongside a fixed invalid ciphertext.
Every ACCEPT (and every REJECT) execution processes identical data. This removes run-to-run data
variance and isolates the path-dependent component from NTT cache noise.

\subsection{Balanced measurement and slot-position controls}
Early two-slot loops exhibited warm-cache/slot-position bias. The final x86 protocol
interleaves ACCEPT and REJECT in a balanced AR/RA order with paired analysis; this
alternation explicitly cancels first-order warm-cache bias. On Raptor Lake, same-ciphertext
(both slots ACCEPT) and slot-swap controls were additionally run to quantify residual
slot-position effects independent of the path.
Algorithm~\ref{alg:balanced} summarises the balanced loop.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Balanced AR/RA measurement loop}
\label{alg:balanced}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require{$N$ iterations, fixed (keypair, ct\_valid, ct\_bad)}
\For{$i = 0$ to $N-1$}
  \If{$i$ is even}  \Comment{AR order}
    \State $t_{A,i} \gets \text{measure}(\textsc{Decaps}(\text{sk}, \text{ct\_valid}))$
    \State $t_{R,i} \gets \text{measure}(\textsc{Decaps}(\text{sk}, \text{ct\_bad}))$
    \State $\delta_i \gets t_{A,i} - t_{R,i}$
  \Else \Comment{RA order}
    \State $t_{R,i} \gets \text{measure}(\textsc{Decaps}(\text{sk}, \text{ct\_bad}))$
    \State $t_{A,i} \gets \text{measure}(\textsc{Decaps}(\text{sk}, \text{ct\_valid}))$
    \State $\delta_i \gets t_{A,i} - t_{R,i}$
  \EndIf
\EndFor
\State Apply Tukey $3\times$IQR filter to $\{\delta_i\}$
\State Report $\bar{\delta}$, Cohen's $d$, bootstrap 95\% CI
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Statistics}
Results report the mean cycle difference $\Delta(\mathrm{A}{-}\mathrm{R})$, Cohen's $d$,
and permutation/bootstrap significance with 95\% confidence intervals. Outliers are removed
with Tukey fences (factor-$3{\times}$ IQR), justified as a conservative choice over the standard
$1.5{\times}$ multiplier to retain valid tail latency without discarding true scheduling noise.
Each condition uses \num{50000}--\num{100000} iterations, yielding bootstrap CIs with ${\sim}1$-cycle precision.
A \emph{two-tier} reporting
policy is adopted: Tier~1 claims concern the measured cycle gap; Tier~2 statements about
microarchitectural cause are framed as ``consistent with'' hypotheses, never as proven attribution.

\subsection{Boundary note}
Direction is boundary-sensitive: at the \emph{internal} \texttt{mlk\_indcpa\_dec} boundary
the sign reverses (REJECT slower, $d\approx{-}0.55$) because that boundary excludes
re-encryption and the FO guard. All Tier~1 claims in this paper are made at the
\emph{public-API} boundary; internal-boundary numbers are not projected onto it.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}
%==============================================================================
\subsection{RQ1: The guard is branchless}
Across all seven tested compiler configurations (GCC and Clang at \texttt{-O2}, \texttt{-O3},
\texttt{-Os} on x86), the disassembly of the conditional-select guard contains no conditional
branch. The guard is realised as a bitwise mask-and-select sequence: the comparison result is
expanded into a full-width mask by an arithmetic shift (\texttt{sar} under GCC, \texttt{neg}
followed by \texttt{shr} under Clang), and an \texttt{xor}/\texttt{and}/\texttt{xor} triplet
selects the output without a data-dependent jump. The only \texttt{jne} instruction present
governs the 32-byte loop counter and is data-independent. The branch-predictor-oracle
hypothesis is ruled out at the source-to-assembly level. Any observed timing signal cannot be
a source-level branch effect.

\subsection{RQ2: Positive separation on Westmere}
At the public-API boundary on Westmere, three independent balanced runs are directionally
consistent: ACCEPT is slower than REJECT by $\Delta = {+}80$ to ${+}84$ cycles, with
$d = {+}0.22$ to ${+}0.39$ and paired bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals excluding zero in
all three runs ($p \approx 0$). Each run draws between \num{50000} and \num{100000} paired samples
under the fixed-data protocol. The separation is repeatable and survives the slot-position
controls. A directionally consistent excess of ACCEPT L1 data-cache misses accompanies it
($d = {+}0.35$; Table~\ref{tab:l1d}).

\subsection{RQ3: The separation is generation-dependent}
Under the identical protocol, source, and compiler, Raptor Lake shows no repeatable
separation (Table~\ref{tab:gen}). All three canonical runs trigger a slot-artifact gate:
the per-slot effect sizes disagree in sign, so the small positive $\Delta$ values
($|d| < 0.016$) cannot be attributed to a path-dependent signal. Same-ciphertext and
slot-swap controls show a slot-position penalty of $\sim$19--34 cycles ($|d|\approx0.03$),
comparable to or larger than the balanced signal itself, and falsify a warm-cache
explanation. The conservative verdict is no repeatable separation on Raptor Lake.
The trustworthiness of this null verdict is supported by the slot-swap and
same-ciphertext controls, which quantify the slot-position effect independently
of the path-dependent signal.

\subsection{Performance-counter evidence}
Per-iteration L1 data-cache miss counts (Table~\ref{tab:l1d}) align with the timing result.
On Westmere, ACCEPT incurs $\sim$6 more L1D misses than REJECT ($d = {+}0.35$), directionally
consistent with ACCEPT being slower. On Raptor Lake the per-path difference is negligible
($d = {-}0.03$). Critically, Westmere's absolute L1D miss rate ($\sim$152/iter) is
roughly $58\times$ Raptor Lake's ($\sim$2.6/iter): a fundamentally different cache-pressure
regime. The two cores differ along the axes that govern this regime: Westmere pairs a
32\,KB 8-way L1D with conservative prefetch and an ${\sim}96$-entry out-of-order window,
whereas Raptor Lake pairs a 48\,KB 12-way L1D with aggressive prefetch and an
${\sim}512$-entry window~\cite{intel_sdm}. (LLC and dTLB counters returned zero on both
platforms, a hardware-counter limitation.)

\subsection{Localization and its limit}
Sub-step isolation on Westmere identifies the decompression routine as the largest
isolated contributor, with the \texttt{unpack\_ct} step isolating to $\Delta = {-}35$~cy.
The sum of sub-step deltas (${+}68$~cy, ACCEPT slower)
contradicts the full-function direction (${-}37$~cy at the internal boundary). Sub-step
isolation does not faithfully decompose the full-path signal because the harness alters cache
footprint and inter-step dependencies. The contradiction is a measurement artifact, not a data error.
Each sub-step boundary is delimited by serialising \texttt{RDTSC} instructions;
these introduce memory-ordering barriers absent from full-path execution, altering
the cache footprint and pipeline state at the start of each subsequent step.
The per-step deltas therefore cannot be treated as independent additive contributions
to the full-function timing gap.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
%==============================================================================
A repeatable ACCEPT/REJECT timing separation of ${-}37$ cycles (Cohen's $d = {-}0.55$
at the internal \texttt{mlk\_indcpa\_dec} boundary) was measured on Westmere under verified
branchless assembly. The separation is statistically significant at the level reported
in Section~\ref{sec:results}. Performance-counter data shows a $58\times$ L1D-miss-rate
gap between Westmere and Raptor Lake under ACCEPT conditions. This gap is \emph{consistent with}
a generation-dependent L1D capacity-pressure mechanism, given the 32\,KB vs.\ 48\,KB private
cache difference and the difference in hardware-prefetch aggressiveness~\cite{intel_sdm}.
Source-level constant-time guarantees do not automatically propagate to the microarchitectural
level on all hardware generations.

The same protocol applied to Raptor Lake produced no repeatable separation ($|d| < 0.016$).
This null result is \emph{consistent with} the larger L1D cache and more aggressive prefetcher
on Raptor Lake masking the data-dependent working-set asymmetry.
The timing property is therefore generation-dependent. Deployments
on legacy processor generations face a different risk profile than deployments on current
microarchitectures.

A directional inversion was observed between sub-step totals (${+}68$ cycles, ACCEPT slower)
and the full-function result (${-}37$ cycles, REJECT slower). This inversion is \emph{consistent with}
the serialising \texttt{RDTSC} instructions inserted at sub-step boundaries altering the cache
footprint and pipeline state absent from full-path execution. The per-step measurements are an
observer-effect artifact of the instrumentation harness. Instrumented binaries provide
localisation indicators only; they cannot serve as authoritative sources for end-to-end
timing claims.

The hardware-contingent nature of the constant-time property has deployment implications.
Long-lifecycle deployments on legacy processor generations are at greater risk than deployments
on recent microarchitectures. Attribution of the signal to L1D capacity pressure remains an
unproven hypothesis requiring further controlled testing; Intel Coffee Lake (32\,KB L1D,
post-2017) is the natural next comparison target.

%==============================================================================
\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
%==============================================================================
A repeatable ACCEPT/REJECT timing separation nonetheless persists on Intel Westmere but not
on Intel Raptor Lake under an identical protocol. This is the first cross-generation timing
characterisation of ML-KEM on x86. Performance-counter evidence ($58\times$ L1D-miss-rate
gap; ACCEPT-correlated misses on Westmere) is consistent with a generation-dependent L1D
cache-pressure mechanism, stated as a hypothesis rather than proven attribution. Three
limitations bound these findings. The scope is Westmere-specific under a fixed-data
protocol. The sub-step isolation
artifact (${+}68$~cy sum contradicting the ${-}37$~cy full-function result) prevents
reliable signal decomposition. Future work could include a Coffee Lake measurement
(32\,KB L1D in a newer generation) to test the cache-capacity hypothesis; a survey of
intermediate Intel families (Skylake, Cannon Lake); and investigation of whether a software
countermeasure in the decompression routine would eliminate the asymmetry. No claim
of practical exploitability is made.
